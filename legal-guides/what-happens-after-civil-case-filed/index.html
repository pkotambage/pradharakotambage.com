Going to court

What Actually Happens After a Civil Case Is Filed in Sri Lanka?

A practical explanation of the main stages of a civil action — and why the court case may be narrower than the wider dispute between the parties.

By Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 11 September 2026Sri Lanka
Englishසිංහල

People sometimes imagine that once a civil case is filed, the next step is simply to go to court, tell the judge what happened, produce the documents and wait for a decision.

In reality, a civil case usually passes through several stages before the trial itself begins.

Those early stages are not merely formalities. They help determine what exactly the case is about, what each party is claiming, what is genuinely disputed, and what the court will ultimately have to decide.

A very simplified roadmap looks like this:

Plaint → Summons → Answer → Admissions and Issues → Trial and Evidence → Submissions → Judgment → Enforcement or Appeal, where applicable

The exact course of a case can vary. Some actions may also involve interim applications, preliminary objections or other procedural steps before the main trial proceeds.

But understanding this general structure can make civil litigation much less confusing.

1. The case begins with the plaint

A civil action normally begins with the filing of a plaint.

The plaint sets out the plaintiff's case. In practical terms, it tells the court who the parties are, the material facts relied upon, how the dispute arose and what relief the plaintiff is asking the court to grant.

For example, a person may come to a lawyer saying:

“My brother cheated me out of our father's property.”

That may genuinely describe how the client feels about the dispute.

But a court case cannot simply proceed on that statement.

The lawyer has to identify the legally relevant questions.

Who owns the property? What deeds exist? What exactly did the defendant do? What legal right does the plaintiff rely upon? And, importantly, what exactly is the plaintiff asking the court to do?

This is one of the first important distinctions in civil litigation:

the dispute as the client experiences it and the case that ultimately comes before court are not always the same thing.

2. The defendant is summoned and given an opportunity to respond

Once the action has been properly instituted and the necessary procedural requirements are satisfied, summons may be issued to the defendant.

Receiving summons does not mean:

“The court has already decided that I am wrong.”

It means that a claim has been brought against you and that the legal process has begun.

The defendant is then given an opportunity to place his or her position before court.

That response commonly takes the form of an answer.

The defendant may deny the plaintiff's allegations, admit some facts but dispute others, rely on a different version of events, or raise legal objections to the claim.

At this point, the actual dispute before court starts becoming clearer.

3. A plaint and answer are not the same thing as evidence

This is something litigants often misunderstand.

A client may say:

“But we have already written everything in the plaint.”

Or:

“Everything in his answer is false. How can the court allow him to say that?”

The plaint and answer are extremely important because they define the cases the parties are putting forward.

But stating something in a pleading is not the same thing as proving a disputed fact at trial.

A simple way to understand the difference is:

Pleadings tell the court what each party says the case is. Evidence is what the parties later use to prove disputed facts.

At the same time, matters admitted by the parties may not need to be proved in the same way as matters that remain disputed.

This is why the pleadings matter so much before the trial begins.

They help identify what actually requires proof.

4. “Why are we wasting time before starting the trial?”

This is a common frustration.

Clients sometimes ask:

“Why can't we just start the trial?”
“Everybody knows what the dispute is.”
“Why are there so many steps before witnesses are called?”

The concern is understandable.

Civil litigation can involve practical delays, and not every delay should be confused with the legal purpose of the procedural stages themselves.

But the preliminary stages of a case do serve an important function.

Before evidence is led, the court and the parties need to know what is admitted, what is denied, what facts genuinely require proof, what legal questions arise and what the court must ultimately decide.

These steps help give the trial its boundaries.

Without those boundaries, each side could simply tell years of history, produce piles of documents and complain about every wrong they believe the other person committed.

That is not the purpose of a civil trial.

5. The issues identify what the court actually has to decide

One of the most important stages is the identification of the issues in the case.

In simple terms, an issue is a disputed question that has to be determined in order to decide the case.

Imagine a land dispute where the plaintiff says:

“This land belongs to me.”

The defendant replies:

“No. I have acquired rights to it.”

Behind those two statements may be several different questions.

Does the plaintiff have the title claimed? What is the defendant's legal basis for resisting the claim? Have the necessary facts been established? Is the plaintiff entitled to the relief requested?

The issues help turn a broad human dispute into specific questions that the court can answer.

That is why a court case can sometimes appear much narrower than the conflict the parties have been fighting about for years.

6. The dispute between the parties and the court case are not always the same thing

This is particularly important in family, land and long-running personal disputes.

The client's real concern may be:

“After everything I did for him, he betrayed me.”
“My sister always received more from our parents.”
“He insulted me in front of everyone.”
“They have been trying to push me out for years.”

Those experiences may be completely genuine.

They may explain why the dispute became so serious.

But the court can decide only the legal questions properly placed before it.

A civil case is not necessarily an inquiry into everything that has ever gone wrong between the parties.

This is sometimes difficult for litigants to accept because, emotionally, they may want the entire history to be heard and recognised.

As lawyers conducting the litigation, however, we have to keep returning to a more focused question:

What facts and legal rights actually matter to this case?

7. Then the trial begins

Once the case reaches trial, evidence is led on the matters that require determination.

Witnesses may give evidence. The opposing party normally has an opportunity to test that evidence through cross-examination. Documents and other material may also become relevant depending on the case.

The other side may then present its own evidence.

This is where litigants often begin to experience another kind of anxiety.

A witness for the other side may confidently say something the client believes is completely false.

A document may appear damaging when first produced.

A witness may forget an important date.

A particular answer in cross-examination may sound very good or very bad.

But a civil trial should not be judged one sentence at a time.

8. Evidence is assessed together — not one sentence at a time

Clients sometimes become very worried when the opposing party gives evidence which they believe is false.

They may immediately think:

“He said that under oath. Now the judge will believe him.”

But evidence given by a witness is not automatically final or conclusive merely because it has been stated in court.

That evidence may be tested in cross-examination.

It may be compared with documents.

There may be contradictions with previous statements.

Other witnesses may give different evidence.

The surrounding circumstances may make parts of the story more or less probable.

Likewise, one apparently damaging answer does not necessarily destroy an entire case.

The court ultimately has to consider the evidence relevant to the issues as a whole.

So litigation should not be viewed as:

“Their witness answered that question well, therefore we are losing.”

or:

“Our witness forgot one thing, therefore the case is finished.”

The case has to be assessed in its full evidentiary context.

9. Not every document you have automatically proves your case

Another common misunderstanding is:

“I have all the documents. Surely that means I will win.”

Not necessarily.

A client may bring electricity bills, bank records, photographs, messages, receipts, old deeds, tax documents or handwritten notes.

Those documents may be important.

But the real question is not simply:

“Do we have a document?”

It is:

“What particular fact does this document actually help us prove?”

A bank transfer may establish that money changed hands, but not necessarily why it was paid.

An electricity bill may support an argument about occupation, but it does not automatically establish ownership of land.

A photograph may show the condition of something at a particular time, but it does not necessarily prove every allegation connected with it.

Documents have to be considered in relation to the issues that actually arise in the case.

10. After the evidence, the parties may make submissions

Once the evidence has been concluded, the lawyers may address the court on how the evidence and the applicable law should be considered.

Depending on the case and the directions of court, submissions may be oral, written, or both.

Submissions are not an opportunity to introduce an entirely new factual case.

Their purpose is to explain how the evidence already before court and the applicable law support the position taken by the party.

The court then considers the case and delivers its judgment.

11. Judgment does not always mean the entire dispute is over

The judgment determines the issues before court and whether the plaintiff is entitled to the relief claimed.

But even a successful judgment may not immediately produce the practical result the successful party expects.

For example, obtaining a judgment ordering the payment of money does not necessarily mean that the money will be handed over the next day.

Further enforcement procedures may sometimes be necessary.

A dissatisfied party may also, depending on the circumstances and applicable procedure, have a right to challenge the decision before a higher court.

Those are separate stages with their own procedures.

A civil case cannot resolve every part of a human conflict

Perhaps one of the hardest realities of litigation is that a court case cannot necessarily resolve the entire emotional conflict between the parties.

A court may decide who owns the land.

It may decide whether money is owed.

It may determine whether a particular legal relief should be granted.

But a judgment cannot necessarily repair a broken sibling relationship, years of resentment, humiliation within a family, feelings of betrayal or the emotional cost of a long-running dispute.

As lawyers, we can present and argue the legal case.

But there are parts of a human conflict that litigation is simply not designed to resolve.

That is also one reason why negotiation, settlement or mediation may sometimes be worth considering in appropriate disputes.

So what actually happens after a civil case is filed?

A civil action is not simply:

File case → tell your story → judge decides.

It is a structured process.

The pleadings identify the cases of the parties.

The disputed matters are narrowed.

The issues identify what the court must decide.

Evidence is then presented and tested in relation to those issues.

The lawyers make the necessary legal submissions.

The court considers the evidence and the law before delivering judgment.

Understanding that process can make litigation less confusing.

More importantly, it helps a litigant understand something fundamental:

The purpose of a civil trial is not to prove that the other person is a bad person. It is to establish the facts and legal rights that matter to the particular case before the court.
