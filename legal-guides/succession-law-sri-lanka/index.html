What Happens to a Person's Property After Death in Sri Lanka?

A clear introduction to inheritance, last wills and the legal steps families may need to take.

Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 6 November 2026Sri Lanka
Englishසිංහල

When someone dies, what happens to the house they lived in, the land they owned, and the money in their bank accounts? Does everything belong to the surviving husband or wife? Are the children entitled to equal shares? What happens if the person never made a last will? These are questions many Sri Lankan families face, often during an already difficult time. The answers depend on succession law.

What Is Succession?

Succession is the process through which a deceased person's property passes to those legally entitled to receive it. The property and relevant financial obligations left behind are generally called the deceased's estate.

An estate may include houses, land, vehicles, bank accounts, investments, company shares and valuable belongings. However, a person can generally pass on only property or legal interests that actually belonged to them. If they owned half a house, the other half does not become part of their estate simply because they died.

Some assets may need separate examination because of nominations, insurance arrangements or other legally recognised interests. The first step is to find out exactly what property the deceased owned.

What Happens If There Is No Last Will?

When someone dies without a legally effective will covering their property, the applicable law decides who inherits. This is called intestate succession.

Suppose a father dies leaving a wife, two children and a house, but no will. The family cannot simply assume the house belongs entirely to the mother or eldest child. Under Sri Lanka's general law, the surviving spouse ordinarily receives one-half of the deceased's intestate property, and the remainder passes to the other entitled heirs. Special inheritance laws may lead to a different result.

What Happens If There Is a Last Will?

A person may make a last will identifying who should receive property after death. This is known as testate succession. A mother, for example, might leave her house to one child and another property to someone else.

Sri Lankan law recognises broad freedom to decide how property should be distributed by a valid will. But the will must meet legal requirements, and the estate must be administered through the applicable process.

Does Everyone Follow the Same Inheritance Law?

No. Sri Lanka has a general inheritance law and special systems including Kandyan law, Thesawalamai and Muslim law. Which applies depends on legally relevant circumstances. The same inheritance formula cannot safely be used for every family.

What Happens to the Deceased's Debts?

Loans, mortgages and other legally enforceable liabilities must be considered before distribution. A house worth Rs. 20 million may still be subject to a mortgage. Relatives do not ordinarily become personally responsible for every debt merely by inheriting, although separate liability may arise for a co-borrower or guarantor.

Does the Family Have to Go to Court?

Depending on the estate, probate, letters of administration or certificates of heirship may be necessary. These documents address authority to administer the estate or recognition of the heirs. A legal right to inherit does not automatically mean a person can immediately sell the inherited land.

The Civil Procedure Code regulates important testamentary proceedings. Court documentation and the right to inherit are related, but they are not the same question.

What Should the Family Do First?

  1. Locate any last will.
  2. Identify the deceased's property and debts.
  3. Gather deeds, financial records, birth, marriage and death certificates.
  4. Identify possible heirs and the law that applies.
  5. Check whether court proceedings are needed before transferring property.

Conclusion

Inheritance is not always a simple division among surviving relatives. Whether a will exists, which law applies, who survives the deceased and what property they owned can all change the outcome. Understanding these matters early helps families protect their rights and avoid unnecessary disputes.
