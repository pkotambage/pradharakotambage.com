What Property Forms Part of a Deceased Person's Estate in Sri Lanka?

Before calculating inheritance shares, find out which assets the deceased actually owned.

Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 6 November 2026Sri Lanka

When someone dies, family members often begin discussing who should inherit the house, land or savings. But before deciding who receives what, there is an important question: what property actually belonged to the deceased? Not everything connected to a deceased person necessarily forms part of the property available for inheritance. Understanding this distinction can help prevent misunderstandings and disputes.

What Is Included in an Estate?

When someone dies, relatives often ask who should inherit the house or savings. But the first question is what property actually belonged to the deceased?

An estate may include land, houses, vehicles, bank balances, investments, company shares, valuables and business rights. Ownership, mortgages, trusts and nominations may change the position.

It may also include money owed to the deceased and certain rights connected with a business. Not every asset associated with the deceased forms part of the estate that can be divided immediately. Establish the legal basis of ownership.

What If the House Belonged to Both Husband and Wife?

Suppose a husband and wife own a house worth Rs. 20 million in equal shares. The husband dies without a will, leaving his wife and two children. The wife's existing half-share does not become part of the husband's estate. Only the husband's half-share is inherited.

Assuming the general law applies and there are no complicating circumstances:

PersonFinal share of house
Wife3/4
First child1/8
Second child1/8

The wife keeps her own half and inherits half of her husband's half. This is why the deeds must be checked first.

What If the Deceased Gave Property Away Before Death?

If a father transferred land to his daughter through a legally effective deed of gift, that land may no longer be part of his estate. The deed and retained interests must be examined.

Section 35 of the Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance also includes a rule called collation. Certain substantial lifetime benefits given to a child to establish them in life may need to be taken into account in calculating inheritance. Not every small gift is covered; the statute contains qualifications and exceptions.

What About Money in Bank Accounts?

Some deposits form part of the estate, but a valid nomination can change the treatment. Section 544 of the Civil Procedure Code covers nominations of specified financial assets, including qualifying bank deposits and other interests. A nomination may take effect despite a different instruction in a will.

For example, a validly nominated daughter may receive a qualifying deposit under the nomination rather than the ordinary division of the estate. Not all accounts are treated alike; the relevant account and nomination must be examined.

In particular, it is important to distinguish between an ordinary bank balance and a financial interest covered by a valid statutory nomination. The relevant account terms and documents must be reviewed.

What About Insurance and Business Assets?

Section 554A of the Civil Procedure Code excludes certain insurance money and nominated financial assets from estate property for testamentary proceedings. Company shares owned personally by the deceased may form part of the estate; assets owned by the company itself do not automatically become personal property of the shareholder. Partnership agreements and other documents may also matter.

A business partnership may require examination of the partnership agreement and the deceased's interest in it. Insurance proceeds may be paid under their own statutory and contractual arrangements.

Do Debts Affect the Estate?

Yes. Mortgages, loans and other enforceable obligations must be considered before distribution. An heir should not assume inherited property is free of security or other liabilities.

What Documents Should Be Collected?

Preserve deeds, survey plans, financial and investment records, nomination forms, insurance documents, vehicle registrations, loan agreements and any will. Avoid transferring or disposing of assets before establishing their legal position.

A property investigation or title search may be necessary where the ownership history is uncertain. Avoid assuming that possession of a deed proves exclusive ownership.

Conclusion

Identify the estate before dividing it. Joint ownership, lifetime transfers, nominations, business interests and debts can all alter what is actually available for inheritance.

Legal References

  • Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance, sections 21, 22 and 35
  • Civil Procedure Code, sections 544 and 554A
