Family Law

Divorce Without Unnecessary Conflict: Understanding Your Legal Options in Sri Lanka

A practical introduction to lawful divorce, children, financial arrangements and reducing avoidable conflict.

Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-Law26 September 2026Sri Lanka
Englishසිංහල

1. When a marriage may be coming to an end

Marriage is both a personal relationship and a legal bond. Sometimes spouses reconcile; in other cases, one or both conclude that continuing the relationship is no longer possible. The prospect of court proceedings, family involvement, financial uncertainty and decisions about children can feel overwhelming. Understanding the law and discussing practical arrangements early may reduce avoidable conflict. A quieter approach does not mean giving up legal rights.

2. Can both spouses simply agree to divorce?

Under Sri Lanka’s general marriage law, mutual consent alone is not a standalone ground for divorce. Section 19 of the Marriage Registration Ordinance recognises adultery after marriage, malicious desertion and incurable impotence at the time of marriage. The relevant ground must be properly pleaded and established. The position may differ under Kandyan and Muslim personal laws.

For a focused explanation, see Can Both Spouses Agree to Divorce in Sri Lanka?.

3. Does the case have to become a contested battle?

Not necessarily. Where the spouses agree that the marriage should end, they may be able to narrow issues concerning financial claims, property and children. A defendant may choose not to contest the relief sought, but that does not make divorce automatic: the court must still be satisfied on the legal ground and evidence. Pleadings and testimony must be truthful; an agreement cannot justify invented allegations.

4. What if there are children?

Parents can discuss who will care for the children, where they will live, contact with the other parent, education, healthcare and financial support. Sometimes one parent has custody with reasonable access for the other; in other circumstances, shared arrangements are proposed. The children's welfare remains central, and agreed arrangements are subject to the applicable law and court oversight. Separation does not end parental responsibilities.

5. Can financial matters be settled amicably?

A couple may agree on their positions regarding maintenance, property, liabilities and any damages claims, and reflect appropriate terms in the proceedings. But not every financial right or judicial power disappears merely because spouses agree. Each should understand the legal effect of a settlement before accepting it, especially where children’s support is concerned.

6. Can mediation help?

Mediation may help spouses discuss practical arrangements in a structured, less confrontational setting, particularly parenting and financial issues. It does not itself dissolve a marriage or replace divorce proceedings. Where domestic violence, coercion or an imbalance of power makes voluntary participation unsafe, mediation may be unsuitable.

7. When is divorce final?

The first decree is a decree nisi. Under sections 604 and 605 of the Civil Procedure Code, it ordinarily cannot be made absolute before at least three months, or a longer period prescribed by the court. Special timing rules apply where the decree was entered ex parte. The marriage is finally dissolved when the decree becomes absolute; the parties should not assume that an initial decree permits remarriage.

8. Before proceeding

Understand the applicable marriage law, the grounds actually available, the relief requested, documents and evidence, children’s needs and financial consequences. Independent legal advice is useful even when both spouses agree. The aim is not to encourage or discourage divorce, but to make informed choices while keeping unnecessary conflict to a minimum.
