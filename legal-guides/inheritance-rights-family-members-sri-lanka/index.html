Can Parents, Brothers, Sisters and Grandchildren Inherit Property in Sri Lanka?

Practical examples of inheritance rights beyond the surviving spouse and children.

Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 6 November 2026Sri Lanka

When someone dies without a last will, the surviving husband, wife and children are usually the first family members considered. But what if the deceased had no children? Can their parents inherit? Do brothers and sisters have rights? What happens when a child died before their parent, leaving grandchildren? Sri Lanka's general inheritance law provides rules for these situations.

Who Has the First Right to Inherit?

Under general intestate succession law, children and other descendants take priority over parents, siblings and more distant relatives, subject to the surviving spouse's statutory rights. Certain exceptions and rules of representation apply.

The statutory order is important: a more distant relative cannot ordinarily inherit alongside a closer category where the law gives that category priority. The surviving spouse's entitlement must also be considered.

Can Grandchildren Inherit?

Yes. Suppose a grandmother had two children: a daughter who is alive and a son who died before her, leaving two children. The grandmother dies without a spouse or will. Under general law, the daughter ordinarily receives one-half and the two grandchildren share the deceased son's half, receiving one-quarter each.

This is called inheritance by representation. Grandchildren do not automatically take equal shares alongside their living parent.

What If Both Parents of the Deceased Are Alive?

If a married man dies with no descendants but leaves his wife and both parents, under general law his wife ordinarily receives one-half and the father and mother one-quarter each. This assumes the full estate is subject to general intestacy.

What If Only One Parent Survives?

Under section 25 of the Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance, one surviving parent may share with qualifying siblings of the deceased. For example, where a person dies leaving no spouse or descendants, but a mother and one full brother, the mother would ordinarily receive half and the brother the other half.

The result may change where full or half-siblings or descendants of deceased siblings are involved. Section 25 includes a qualification where no qualifying sibling is alive at the deceased's death: in that situation the surviving parent may take the whole relevant portion even if descendants of deceased siblings survive.

This is why it is important to examine exactly who was alive on the date of death and to distinguish siblings of the whole blood from certain half-siblings.

Can Brothers and Sisters Inherit?

Yes, where the statutory order permits it. Sections 26–29 distinguish full siblings from half-siblings and make particular provision for paternal and maternal relationships. Equal division among everyone called a sibling may therefore be wrong.

What About Uncles, Aunts and More Distant Relatives?

More distant relatives may inherit when closer statutory classes have failed. Section 31 deals with remoter ascendants and collateral relatives; further provisions address cases where no qualifying heirs remain. Exact relationships must be established.

Where no qualifying relatives remain, the law also makes provision for the ultimate destination of the property. A person's closeness in everyday family life does not alone determine succession rights.

Can an Adopted Child Inherit?

A valid adoption can give important inheritance rights. Section 6 of the Adoption of Children Ordinance generally treats an adopted child as a child born in lawful wedlock of the adopter, subject to statutory qualifications. Particular inheritance issues involving the adopter's wider family, older instruments or special laws require separate consideration.

What About Children Born Outside Marriage?

Section 33 of the Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance uses historical statutory terminology and contains particular rules affecting maternal and paternal intestate succession. Legal parentage, other legislation and the applicable legal system must be examined. It would be misleading to assume all cases produce identical rights without a legal assessment.

What If the Family Disagrees?

Gather birth, marriage, adoption and death records, establish all relevant relationships and apply the correct law before asserting shares or transferring property.

Uncertain parentage, missing documents and disputes about a deceased sibling's descendants may require closer investigation. It is safer to establish the family tree and legal entitlement before making property transfers.

Conclusion

Parents, siblings, grandchildren and more distant relatives can inherit in appropriate cases, but closeness of relationship alone does not settle entitlement. The statutory order matters.
