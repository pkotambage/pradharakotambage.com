Does Everyone Inherit Property Under the Same Law in Sri Lanka?

Why similar families may inherit different shares under Sri Lanka's legal systems.

Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 6 November 2026Sri Lanka

Two Sri Lankan families may experience similar circumstances. In both families, a father dies without leaving a last will. Each father leaves a wife, children and land. But does the law necessarily divide their property in the same way? No. Sri Lanka has different legal systems governing inheritance. This is important to understand before calculating shares in a deceased person's property.

Why Are There Different Inheritance Laws?

Two families may each have a father who died without a will leaving a wife, children and land, yet the same inheritance formula may not apply to both. Sri Lanka's law developed through general law, customary laws and legislation. The principal systems are general law, Kandyan law, Thesawalamai and Muslim law.

These rules developed from a combination of general law, customary law and legislation. They should not be treated as interchangeable, and the relevant legal system should be identified at the beginning of the inquiry.

1. General Law of Inheritance

The Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance provides the general intestate rules. A surviving spouse ordinarily receives one-half, with children and other descendants taking priority in distributing the remainder. For example, a wife and two children ordinarily receive one-half, one-quarter and one-quarter respectively, assuming the general law governs the distributable estate.

2. Kandyan Law

Kandyan law contains distinct inheritance rules under legislation including the Kandyan Law Declaration and Amendment Ordinance and the Kandyan Succession Ordinance. It distinguishes certain categories of property, including paraveni ancestral or inherited immovable property and acquired property.

It also contains rules regarding surviving spouses' life interests and, in specific contexts, binna and diga marriages. A life interest generally permits enjoyment or use during a person's lifetime without necessarily granting full ownership. These provisions require individual application, not the automatic use of the general-law one-half spouse rule.

Residence in the Central Province alone does not establish that a deceased person was governed by Kandyan law. Its application must be legally determined.

3. Thesawalamai

The Jaffna Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance provides important rules for persons governed by Thesawalamai. The origin and category of property, including ancestral interests, may affect inheritance; in some circumstances a link to the father's or mother's side matters.

Not every Tamil person is automatically governed by Thesawalamai. Its applicability is a legal question, not a conclusion based only on ethnicity, surname or present address.

In particular, property associated with different family lines may be dealt with differently under the relevant provisions. It is necessary to examine the person's status as well as the origin and character of the property.

4. Muslim Law

The Muslim Intestate Succession Ordinance provides for the application of the Muslim law governing the sect of the deceased where its statutory conditions are satisfied. It has distinct rules concerning spouses, children, parents and other relatives. Do not apply the general-law half-share formula automatically to a Muslim estate.

What If the Deceased Left a Will?

The Wills Ordinance, as amended in 2022, recognises broad testamentary freedom, but a will's validity and legal effects must be considered under the law relevant to the particular circumstances.

Intestate succession rules should not simply be used to calculate the distribution where a legally effective will disposes of the relevant property.

What If the Deceased Lived Abroad?

International estates may involve additional rules. Under section 21 of the Matrimonial Rights and Inheritance Ordinance, succession to immovable property situated in Sri Lanka is distinguished from movable property, for which domicile may matter. Domicile is not necessarily the same as nationality or temporary residence.

How Can the Family Identify the Applicable Law?

Consider the deceased's legally relevant status, family and marriage history, domicile, type and location of property, and any will. Do not calculate shares merely from religion, surname or present address.

Conclusion

There is no single intestate inheritance formula for every Sri Lankan family. Establish the applicable law before deciding who inherits and in what proportions.
