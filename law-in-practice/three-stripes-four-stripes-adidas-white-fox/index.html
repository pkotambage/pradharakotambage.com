Three Stripes, Four Stripes: Understanding the Trademark Dispute Between Adidas and White Fox Boutique

Can one extra stripe make the difference? A legal and commercial mediation perspective on the Adidas–White Fox dispute.

By Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 15 October 2026

Can adding one extra stripe to a clothing design prevent trademark infringement?

This seemingly simple question is at the centre of a legal dispute between German sportswear manufacturer Adidas and Australian fashion retailer White Fox Boutique.

Adidas has commenced proceedings in the Federal Court of Australia, alleging that White Fox's use of four parallel stripes on certain clothing items infringes its well-known three-stripe trademark.

White Fox disputes the allegation, maintaining that its four-stripe design is decorative rather than a trademark.

Beyond the immediate dispute, the proceedings raise broader questions concerning trademark protection, commercial competition, consumer perception and the boundaries of intellectual property rights.

The case also provides an interesting opportunity to examine how legal positions and underlying commercial interests may differ, particularly from the perspective of negotiation and mediation.

1. Background to the Dispute

Adidas has used its three-stripe design for decades, developing it into one of the most recognisable features of its commercial identity.

According to an ABC News report published on 12 October 2026, Adidas alleges that White Fox Boutique has promoted and supplied clothing featuring four parallel stripes since at least March 2026.

The disputed designs reportedly involve four stripes of equal width in a colour contrasting with the clothing.

Adidas contends that the designs are deceptively similar to its registered three-stripe trademark.

The company also alleges that White Fox used genuine Adidas footwear in promotional photographs alongside clothing bearing the disputed four-stripe design. Adidas argues that this could encourage consumers to associate the two brands.

According to the reported proceedings, Adidas requested White Fox to cease using the disputed design and remove the relevant products from sale. White Fox declined.

White Fox's legal representatives have argued that the four stripes are decorative elements and have referred to other fashion retailers using similar striped designs.

The dispute is currently before the Federal Court of Australia, with a hearing scheduled for early 2027.

The allegations have not yet been finally determined.

2. What Does Trademark Law Protect?

A trademark primarily serves to distinguish the goods or services of one business from those of another.

Although trademarks are commonly associated with business names and logos, protection may also extend to distinctive symbols, shapes, patterns and other visual features.

In the present dispute, Adidas relies on trademark protection associated with its three-stripe design.

The relevant legislation is Australia's Trade Marks Act 1995 (Cth).

Under section 120(1), infringement may occur where a person uses, as a trademark, a sign that is substantially identical with or deceptively similar to a registered trademark in relation to goods or services covered by the registration.

Two matters are therefore particularly important:

  1. Whether the allegedly infringing design is being used as a trademark.
  2. Whether the design is substantially identical with, or deceptively similar to, the registered trademark.

Trademark protection does not necessarily confer an unrestricted monopoly over every design incorporating similar visual elements.

Its scope must be determined by reference to the registered rights, the allegedly infringing use and the applicable legal principles.

3. Does the Number of Stripes Determine Infringement?

One of the most interesting aspects of the dispute is that Adidas uses three stripes, while White Fox uses four.

Does the addition of a fourth stripe sufficiently distinguish the designs?

The answer is not necessarily.

Section 10 of Australia's Trade Marks Act 1995 addresses deceptive similarity. The statutory test concerns whether a sign so nearly resembles another trademark that it is likely to deceive or cause confusion.

The assessment is not limited to a mechanical comparison of individual design features.

The overall impression created by the competing signs is important, including how an ordinary consumer may recall a trademark imperfectly.

For example, a consumer may remember the general appearance of a distinctive striped design without necessarily recalling every detail.

However, the presence of similar stripes does not automatically establish trademark infringement.

Differences in appearance, presentation and commercial context may also be legally significant.

Consequently, neither the existence of a fourth stripe nor the general resemblance between the designs can independently determine the outcome.

The court must consider the relevant evidence and apply the statutory requirements to the particular designs before it.

4. Decorative Design or Trademark Use?

White Fox's argument that its four-stripe design is merely decorative raises a significant legal distinction.

Clothing commonly incorporates visual features such as stripes, patterns, contrasting colours and geometric arrangements.

These elements may be used simply to improve the appearance of a garment.

However, a visual feature may also function as a trademark when it distinguishes the goods of one business from those of its competitors.

The important question is not merely whether a design is attractive or decorative.

It is whether the design, in its particular commercial context, is being used as a sign indicating the commercial origin of the goods.

A design may have aesthetic qualities while also performing a trademark function.

Conversely, the presence of a familiar visual feature does not necessarily mean it is being used as a trademark.

This distinction is likely to be important in considering the parties' competing arguments.

5. Why Does the Advertising Matter?

The dispute extends beyond the four-stripe design itself.

Adidas alleges that White Fox displayed genuine Adidas footwear alongside White Fox clothing in certain advertisements.

This raises an additional question concerning how consumers may perceive the relationship between the two brands.

Advertising can influence consumer perceptions of commercial origin, endorsement, sponsorship or affiliation.

Where products bearing similar visual features are presented together, the surrounding promotional context may become relevant to the legal assessment.

Nevertheless, the appearance of products from different brands in the same advertisement does not automatically establish an infringement or misleading commercial association.

Any such allegation must be established by reference to the applicable law and evidence.

This aspect of the dispute demonstrates that trademark litigation may involve not only the appearance of a product but also the manner in which it is marketed.

6. Previous Trademark Disputes Involving Adidas

Adidas v Pacific Brands — Australia

In Adidas AG v Pacific Brands Footwear Pty Ltd (No 3) [2013] FCA 905, the Federal Court of Australia considered alleged trademark infringement involving several footwear designs incorporating four stripes.

Three of the twelve challenged designs were found to be deceptively similar to Adidas's registered trademark.

The different outcomes concerning the designs illustrate the importance of examining the particular appearance and circumstances of each allegedly infringing use.

Adidas v Thom Browne — United States

Adidas also pursued trademark litigation in the United States against fashion designer Thom Browne over striped designs.

The proceedings resulted in an outcome unfavourable to Adidas.

Related litigation concerning Adidas's trademark protection has also occurred in the United Kingdom, where certain registrations were successfully challenged.

These cases illustrate that trademark disputes involving apparently similar designs do not necessarily produce identical outcomes.

The relevant registrations, evidence, legal standards and jurisdiction may differ substantially.

Accordingly, previous judgments provide useful legal context, but they do not predetermine the result of the current Australian proceedings.

7. The Commercial Interests Behind Trademark Litigation

Trademark litigation is not concerned solely with the legal classification of a particular design.

A recognisable trademark can represent substantial commercial investment.

Businesses may devote significant resources to developing brand identity, advertising, product design and consumer recognition.

Protecting that investment may be commercially important because a distinctive brand can help consumers identify products and distinguish them from competitors.

At the same time, competing businesses have legitimate interests in developing products, following fashion trends and using design features that remain available in the marketplace.

How can the law protect a distinctive brand identity without unnecessarily restricting legitimate competition?

This balance is important because trademark protection serves both private commercial interests and the wider function of distinguishing goods and services in the marketplace.

8. Why This Case Is Interesting from a Mediation Perspective

Beyond the legal questions concerning trademark infringement, this dispute provides an opportunity to examine the distinction between positions and underlying interests, a fundamental concept in commercial mediation.

Intellectual property disputes are not excluded from mediation merely because they concern registered legal rights.

Indeed, the Federal Court of Australia's Intellectual Property Practice Note (IP-1) expressly recognises mediation as an appropriate dispute resolution mechanism in intellectual property proceedings.

Paragraphs 8.1–8.3 of the Practice Note encourage consideration of alternative dispute resolution and confirm that the Court may refer an intellectual property matter to mediation at any stage of proceedings, whether to resolve the entire case or particular issues.

The World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), through its Arbitration and Mediation Center, also facilitates the mediation of international intellectual property disputes, including trademark disputes.

Although a mediator cannot independently determine trademark validity with binding effect on third parties or revoke statutory rights, the parties may negotiate arrangements concerning the exercise, use and enforcement of those rights, subject to applicable law.

Understanding Positions and Interests

A position describes what a party says it wants.

An interest helps explain why that outcome matters to the party.

Based on the publicly reported allegations and responses, the parties' positions and possible underlying interests may be illustrated as follows:

PartyReported legal positionPossible underlying commercial interests
AdidasPrevent the allegedly infringing use of the disputed four-stripe design.Brand protection, preservation of trademark distinctiveness, consumer recognition, exclusivity associated with its registered rights and commercial reputation.
White Fox BoutiqueDefend its ability to use the four-stripe design.Commercial freedom, avoiding redesign costs, protecting existing inventory, maintaining sales and preserving flexibility in future clothing designs.

These underlying interests are hypothetical interpretations for the purpose of mediation analysis. They should not be treated as established facts concerning the parties' motivations or priorities.

Moving Beyond Legal Positions

If the dispute is approached exclusively through opposing legal positions, the available outcomes may appear relatively narrow.

Adidas seeks protection against what it considers trademark infringement.

White Fox maintains that its design is lawful and should remain available for use.

A court may ultimately determine the legal rights and liabilities of the parties according to the applicable law and evidence.

However, commercial mediation introduces a different question:

Is there a commercially acceptable arrangement that could address the important interests of both parties without requiring a final judicial determination?

Exploring that question does not require either party to abandon its legal position.

Instead, it involves examining whether there may be ways to accommodate important commercial concerns through negotiation.

Exploring Possible Settlement Options

Depending on the parties' priorities and willingness to negotiate, hypothetical settlement options might include:

  1. Modification of the stripe arrangement: White Fox could consider changes to the design that create greater visual differentiation while preserving its preferred clothing style.
  2. Changes to advertising practices: The parties could explore promotional arrangements intended to reduce the possibility of consumers assuming a commercial connection between the brands.
  3. Phased withdrawal of particular products: A negotiated transition period could potentially address existing inventory and commercial commitments while accommodating concerns about future use.
  4. An agreed coexistence arrangement: The parties might consider clearly defined boundaries governing the use of particular designs or promotional features.

These are illustrative possibilities, not reported settlement proposals or negotiations between Adidas and White Fox.

Their practical or legal suitability would depend on the circumstances and the parties' agreement.

The Role of a Commercial Mediator

A mediator would not decide whether White Fox's four-stripe design infringes Adidas's registered trademark.

Nor would the mediator determine which party is legally correct.

Instead, the mediator could assist the parties in identifying their concerns, understanding the significance of those concerns, and exploring whether mutually acceptable arrangements are possible.

For example, Adidas might regard preventing consumer confusion and protecting the distinctiveness of its brand as particularly important.

White Fox might attach considerable importance to avoiding an immediate withdrawal of existing products or preserving flexibility in future designs.

A mediator would need to explore these possible interests with the parties rather than assume their existence or relative importance.

The discussion might reveal that certain interests can be accommodated without requiring complete agreement on the underlying legal questions.

This reflects an important principle of interest-based negotiation:

A disagreement over legal rights does not necessarily mean that every underlying commercial interest is incompatible.

Of course, mediation does not guarantee settlement.

Some disputes require judicial determination, particularly where parties seek authoritative clarification of legal rights or where proposed commercial arrangements cannot sufficiently address their concerns.

Nevertheless, exploring underlying interests may reveal possibilities that are less apparent when attention remains confined to opposing legal positions.

The Adidas–White Fox dispute therefore provides a useful illustration of how legal analysis and commercial mediation can approach the same conflict from different, but potentially complementary, perspectives.

9. Conclusion

The trademark dispute between Adidas and White Fox Boutique demonstrates that intellectual property conflicts cannot always be resolved by simply identifying visual similarities or differences between competing designs.

The addition of a fourth stripe may be relevant, but it is not necessarily conclusive.

The court must consider the scope of the registered trademark, the manner in which the disputed design is used, and the applicable legal principles concerning trademark infringement.

At a broader level, the case highlights the need to balance the protection of distinctive commercial identities with the freedom of businesses to compete.

From a dispute resolution perspective, it also illustrates the distinction between the legal positions asserted by parties and the commercial interests that may lie behind those positions.

Courts determine legal rights and liabilities. Mediation, where appropriate, provides an opportunity to explore whether competing interests can be accommodated through a negotiated arrangement.

The two approaches serve different purposes, but they need not be mutually exclusive.

The outcome of the Australian proceedings remains to be seen.
