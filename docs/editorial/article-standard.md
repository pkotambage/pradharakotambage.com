# Editorial Standard for Articles

This document defines the default structure for new content on pradharakotambage.com.

The aim is consistency without making every article feel formulaic. Legal Guides and Dispute Resolution articles use related but distinct structures.

## 1. Legal Guides

Legal Guides should begin with the practical problem a reader is facing and answer it in a clear sequence.

### Default structure

1. **Article title**
   - Phrase as a real-world question or practical problem where possible.
   - Make the Sri Lankan context clear when relevant.

2. **Short dek / introduction**
   - Explain in 1–2 sentences what the reader will understand after reading.
   - Avoid technical legal language at the beginning unless it is necessary.

3. **Immediate orientation**
   - State the core legal or practical distinction early.
   - Clarify common misconceptions before moving into detail.

4. **Step-by-step body**
   - Organise the article around the questions a reader would naturally ask next.
   - Number sections where the subject is procedural or sequential.
   - Prefer practical headings over textbook headings.

5. **Examples and distinctions**
   - Use short examples where they make the legal point easier to understand.
   - Explain what a document, statement, procedure or legal concept does and does not prove.
   - Avoid implying that one rule determines every case.

6. **Practical decision point**
   - Towards the end, help the reader identify the questions they should consider before deciding what to do next.
   - This may be a checklist, summary, or short set of questions.

7. **Related guides**
   - Include 2–4 genuinely related articles.
   - Link upward to the relevant topic hub and back to the complete Legal Guides directory.
   - Prefer a natural next step over a random list.

8. **Author note**
   - Keep the standard professional author description.

9. **Disclaimer**
   - State that the article provides general legal information and is not legal advice for a particular matter.

### Tone

- Practical rather than academic.
- Clear without oversimplifying.
- Explain the law before giving terminology where possible.
- Distinguish legal entitlement from practical outcome.
- Avoid promotional claims and unnecessary calls to contact the author.
- Use "may", "can", "generally", and other qualifications where the result depends on facts or procedure.

## 2. Dispute Resolution Articles

Dispute Resolution articles may be more conceptual and reflective. They should explain one important idea clearly rather than forcing a numbered legal-guide format.

### Default structure

1. **Article title**
   - One clear concept or question.

2. **Short dek / introduction**
   - Explain why the concept matters in mediation, negotiation or conflict.

3. **Core idea early**
   - State the central proposition within the opening paragraphs.

4. **Concept development**
   - Break the idea into 3–6 logical sections.
   - Use headings that describe the reader's learning journey rather than academic categories.

5. **Practical example**
   - Where useful, show how the concept appears in an ordinary workplace, family, commercial or interpersonal dispute.

6. **Mediator / participant perspective**
   - Explain what the idea means for the mediator or the participants.
   - Preserve party self-determination and avoid implying that the mediator creates the solution.

7. **Essential takeaway**
   - End the substantive article with a concise synthesis rather than merely repeating the introduction.

8. **Series navigation / related guides**
   - Link to the previous or next article when the piece belongs to a learning sequence.
   - Link back to the correct Dispute Resolution knowledge cluster.

9. **Author note and disclaimer**
   - Retain the standard professional author note and educational-information disclaimer.

### Tone

- Curious, analytical and accessible.
- Avoid presenting mediation techniques as rigid formulas.
- Distinguish positions, interests, needs, emotions and meaning where relevant.
- Do not portray mediation as inherently superior to litigation or arbitration.
- Make clear when content is educational rather than a statement of legal rules.

## 3. Shared publishing checklist

Before publishing any new article:

- Confirm that the title matches the reader's likely question or learning goal.
- Add a concise meta description and social description.
- Assign the article to the correct topic or knowledge cluster.
- Add internal links to 2–4 relevant existing articles.
- Add a clear path back to the relevant topic hub.
- Add English/Sinhala language links when both versions exist.
- Check legal terminology and dates.
- Check that no sentence unintentionally promises an outcome.
- Use the existing author note and disclaimer pattern.
- Add the article to the Legal Guides directory/search index where applicable.
- Add/update sitemap entries where required.
- Check mobile readability before treating publication as complete.

## 4. Principle

The standard is a framework, not a rigid template.

The article should still follow the logic of the subject. A procedural article may require numbered stages; a conceptual article may need only a few thematic sections. Consistency should help the reader navigate the website without making the writing repetitive.
