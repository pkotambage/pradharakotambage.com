Negotiation vs Mediation: What Is the Difference, and When Can Each Help?

Understand the difference between negotiation and mediation, what a mediator adds, and when each approach may help resolve a dispute.

By Pradhara Kotambage, Attorney-at-LawPublished 14 November 2026Sri Lanka
Read this article in / භාෂාව:Englishසිංහල

“We have already discussed this several times. What difference would a mediator make?”

That is an understandable question. When a disagreement arises, people often begin by speaking directly to each other. They explain what happened, ask for a solution, exchange proposals and try to reach an agreement.

Sometimes, those conversations work. Sometimes, they end with both sides repeating the same demands.

Negotiation and mediation both offer ways to seek agreement. Understanding their relationship can help you decide how to approach a difficult conversation—and when assistance might be useful.

What is negotiation?

Negotiation is a process in which people communicate to try to reach an agreement where their needs, preferences or positions differ.

It happens in everyday life: agreeing on a price, discussing a payment deadline, dividing responsibilities or resolving a disagreement about work.

In a dispute, people may negotiate directly or through representatives, including lawyers. Negotiation can be an informal conversation or a carefully prepared exchange of written proposals.

For example, a supplier asks a customer to pay an outstanding invoice. The customer says some goods were defective and asks for a reduction. They discuss replacement goods, the amount payable and a payment date.

That is negotiation. The parties are trying to find terms they can both accept.

What is mediation?

Mediation introduces an impartial third person—the mediator—to help the parties work towards an agreement.

The mediator helps with communication and the process of exploring a resolution. The parties retain the decision about whether to settle and on what terms. The mediator does not impose a judgment or award. [1]

Mediation can therefore be understood as assisted negotiation. Negotiation continues, with a mediator helping the parties conduct it more productively.

The main differences at a glance

What can a mediator add to a conversation?

When negotiations become difficult, the obstacle may extend beyond the amount of money or the wording of a proposal.

One person may feel ignored. Another may distrust every suggestion. Both may worry that making the first concession will be interpreted as weakness.

A mediator can help the parties communicate when emotions or a damaged relationship make direct discussion difficult. [1]

Depending on the dispute and the approach used, this assistance might involve:

  • Giving each person an opportunity to explain their concerns.
  • Identifying the issues that need to be discussed.
  • Asking questions to clarify assumptions.
  • Exploring the needs behind particular demands.
  • Helping the parties develop and assess possible solutions.

For example, “I need payment immediately” may reflect an urgent need to pay staff. “I will not pay until everything is replaced” may reflect a fear of disappointing customers.

Understanding those concerns can create room for proposals that a repeated exchange of demands has overlooked.

An example: unpaid goods and an unhappy customer

Consider this fictional situation.

A supplier delivers packaging materials to a small business. The customer withholds payment, saying that part of the delivery cannot be used.

The supplier says:

“We delivered your order. You must pay the full invoice.”

The customer replies:

“Your goods caused the problem. We will not pay.”

Through direct negotiation

The parties might inspect the disputed goods, agree how many are defective and arrange a replacement or credit.

If they can exchange information and assess proposals constructively, they may resolve the matter themselves.

With a mediator’s assistance

Suppose their discussions have become personal. The supplier believes the complaint is an excuse to delay payment. The customer feels the supplier has dismissed a serious problem.

A mediator might help them explore questions such as:

  • Which part of the delivery is actually disputed?
  • Is there an amount that both sides accept is payable?
  • What does the customer need to complete pending orders?
  • How quickly could replacements be supplied?
  • Does either party want to continue the business relationship?

Possible options might include payment for the accepted goods, replacement of the disputed items, an agreed credit or a revised delivery arrangement.

These are possibilities for discussion. The mediator does not choose an outcome for the parties, and either party may decide that a proposal is unacceptable.

The value of the process lies in helping them examine the problem and their choices more clearly.

When might direct negotiation be enough?

Direct negotiation may be a practical starting point where:

  • The parties can communicate without intimidation.
  • The issues are reasonably clear.
  • Relevant information can be exchanged.
  • Each side can consider proposals and make informed decisions.
  • There is a workable way to record and carry out an agreement.

A disagreement does not automatically require a mediator. A focused conversation, supported by the necessary documents and advice, may be sufficient.

Preparation matters. Before negotiating, consider what you need, what you can offer, what information is missing and what you would do if agreement cannot be reached.

When might mediation help?

Mediation may be worth considering where conversations have become repetitive, trust has weakened or emotions are obstructing progress. It may also be useful where the parties have an ongoing relationship they would like to preserve. [1]

There is no need to regard mediation only as a last attempt after every other conversation has failed. Parties can consider it earlier, before their disagreement becomes more entrenched. WIPO’s guidance recognises that mediation can be used at different stages of a dispute. [2]

A useful question is:

Would assistance with the conversation help us make better-informed decisions about this dispute?

Does mediation mean meeting halfway?

No. Agreement need not involve splitting a sum equally or accepting half of each demand.

In the supplier example, the central issue may be obtaining usable goods by a particular date. A replacement arrangement could matter more to the customer than a discount.

Equally, the supplier may place greater value on prompt payment of the undisputed amount than on continuing an argument about the whole invoice.

Both negotiation and mediation can explore combinations of terms. The aim is to identify an arrangement the parties are willing to accept.

Can lawyers still be involved?

Yes. Negotiation can take place through lawyers, and mediation can include legal advisers, subject to the rules of the particular process. WIPO’s mediation guidance, for example, expressly contemplates party representatives and consultation with lawyers. [2]

A mediator’s impartial role is different from the role of a lawyer advising one party. Taking part in mediation does not remove the value of understanding your legal position before agreeing to terms.

Are the discussions confidential?

It is important to establish the applicable protections before sharing sensitive information.

Mediation rules often contain confidentiality provisions, but their scope and exceptions depend on the governing law, rules and agreements. WIPO’s rules, for example, contain express confidentiality obligations and exceptions. Those particular rules do not automatically govern every mediation. [3]

Similarly, do not assume that every direct settlement discussion is automatically protected from disclosure or later use.

Are there situations where mediation may be unsuitable?

Yes. A mediator’s presence does not automatically resolve intimidation or a serious imbalance of power. Mediation may be inappropriate or ineffective where a party cannot participate freely or another party is unwilling to engage meaningfully. [1]

The suitability of the process matters as much as the desire to settle. An agreement should result from informed choice, with appropriate advice and safeguards where needed.

What if an agreement is reached?

The terms deserve careful attention, whether agreement follows direct negotiation or mediation.

In the supplier example, “we will replace the goods and you will pay” leaves important questions unanswered. Which goods? By what date? Who pays for transport? When does payment fall due?

A clear agreement should address the practical obligations and what happens if they are not fulfilled.

The legal effect and enforceability of a settlement depend on the applicable law, its terms and any required formalities. Agreement through mediation should not be confused with the mediator issuing a binding decision.

Choosing the next step

Negotiation gives people an opportunity to resolve a disagreement through their own discussions. Mediation adds impartial assistance to that effort.

If the conversation is productive, direct negotiation may be enough. If the discussion keeps returning to the same accusations and demands, mediation may offer a way to examine the issues differently.

In either process, the important questions remain: Do you understand the proposal? Does it address what matters to you? Can it be carried out? Are you willing to agree?
